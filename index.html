    01 — Distribution

    Audiences don't rate on a curve

    If ratings were purely honest signal, you'd expect something close to a bell curve centered on 3 stars, but they aren't. A likely reason: people mostly rate movies they chose to watch and finish, so self-selection skews the distribution upward before any opinion is even formed.

    Also notice the shape: half-star values (0.5, 1.5, 2.5…) are consistently rarer than whole-star values right next to them. Half-star ratings were added to MovieLens after whole stars, so people default to the round number unless a movie specifically feels "in between."

    Is it useful? e.g., anyone building a recommender or interpreting "average rating" needs to know the baseline isn't 3.0 — it's closer to 3.5–4.0, so a movie rated exactly average is still being actively avoided by comparison.

    02 — Trend

    The rating boom

    Rating volume by year — a mix of MovieLens's own user base growing and broader online rating behavior becoming normal through the 2000s.

    MovieLens switched from whole-star to half-star ratings in 2003, and the site has gone through several interface redesigns and promotional pushes since 1997. Each tends to bring a wave of returning or newly-signed-up raters. A falloff after 2008 is plausibly ordinary user-base attrition as rating activity gradually shifted to other platforms (Letterboxd, IMDB, social media), though the very last year in this dataset (2015) is also just mechanically undercounted, since the data was cut off in March rather than December.

    What can you do with it? Sanity-checking any year-over-year comparison elsewhere in this dashboard.

    What we actually agree on

    Each point is one movie: how many people rated it (x) against its average score (y). The top-right cluster — widely seen and well liked — is real consensus. High scores from a handful of viewers (top-left) are a different thing: a niche favorite, not a broad one.

    05 — Category comparison

    Genres, ranked

    Average rating and total rating volume by genre. Documentaries and War films often score higher on average with a smaller, more self-selecting audience; high-volume genres like Comedy and Action reach a broader, more mixed crowd, which tends to average out lower.

    Bar length here is a weighted rating, meaning genres with fewer ratings get pulled toward the overall mean, so a genre with only a few thousand ratings can't look artificially better or worse than one with millions just from small-sample noise. Bar color still shows raw volume.

    Why is it useful? Understanding that "genre X rates higher" often reflects who chooses to watch it, not that the genre is inherently better made.

    Conclusion

    Rating alone is a thin signal

    Across every chapter here, the same idea holds: a single star average collapses too much, and self-selection, sample size, disagreement, and genre context all get flattened into one number. Rating count, spread, genre baseline, and genome traits each recover a piece of information that gets lost. Combined, they're a far better basis for a recommendation or an argument about what's actually good.
